# How this project's choices compare with what endurance teams and the rule-makers do

Read on 2026-11-10, before E-021 was registered. The question was whether each Layer 3 choice matches how strategy is done in endurance racing, so the work holds up when discussed with people from teams.

Teams work with telemetry, tyre and fuel data that public timing doesn't contain. So the useful comparison is whether our choices point the same way as theirs, given less data. Where a source was only seen as a search result and not read, it says so, and it isn't used as evidence.

## Summary

| Our choice | What teams and the rule-makers do | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Representative pace is the median of clean laps, by class and race | The WEC's Balance of Performance (BoP) rates cars on race laps. 2024 used the best 20% of laps. 2025 combines the 10 best laps with the average of the best 60%, to be "more race representative" ([The Race, 17 Apr 2025](https://www.the-race.com/endurance/what-the-wec-is-risking-with-its-hypercar-balancing-measures/); [Equipe43](https://racestats.equipe43.com/understanding-the-new-balance-of-performance-approach/)). The 2017 GTE method used the fastest 60% of distance ([ACO/FIA, 2017](http://www.dailysportscar.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/04/BOPWEC2017_EN.pdf)) | Same idea, different cut. Both drop slow laps and summarise the rest robustly. They trim the slowest 40%. We flag by rule (about 12 to 17% of laps by flag) and take a median. |
| Wet running is flagged and kept out of pace figures | The 2017 BoP method makes no adjustment when "there were abnormal weather conditions" ([ACO/FIA, 2017](http://www.dailysportscar.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/04/BOPWEC2017_EN.pdf)). | E-020 fell short: a wet race (COTA 2025) got through, because the slow-lap test measured each race against itself. The rule-makers dropped whole wet races. E-021 instead measures a race that ran slow as a whole against the weekend's practice pace (D-033), which keeps the dry part of a mixed race. Both keep wet running out. |
| Laps since a stop is not tyre age, and no result is called tyre degradation | Stops often don't change tyres. Toyota changed none at its decisive Fuji 2025 stop, and Alpine changed two ([Autosport](https://www.autosport.com/wec/news/how-an-informed-gamble-enabled-toyota-to-beat-alpine-in-the-fuji-6-hours/10859769/)). The winning Ferrari at Le Mans 2025 ran nine triple stints ([24h-lemans.com](https://24h-lemans.com/en/news/2025-24-hours-of-le-mans-focus-on-af-corses-ferrari-499-p-tyre-strategy-59862)). Porsche's longest set at Le Mans 2015 lasted 54 laps across three refuels ([Porsche, 2016](https://newsroom.porsche.com/dam/jcr:fc372d5c-da00-4e14-a972-8643c44edebf/PM_E_RaceStrategy.pdf)). | Consistent. This is the reason the label matters. |
| Fuel and tyre effects aren't separated, and a near-zero net stint-age slope is plausible | Porsche put the fuel effect at about 2 s per lap between a full and an empty tank (44 kg). It put tyres, adjusted for fuel, at about 1.6 s from best to worst over a 54-lap set, and not always linear ([Porsche, 2016](https://newsroom.porsche.com/dam/jcr:fc372d5c-da00-4e14-a972-8643c44edebf/PM_E_RaceStrategy.pdf)). | Consistent. The two effects pull opposite ways within a fuel stint, which fits E-020's small, uncertain slopes. That's an LMP1 car in 2015, used as an order of magnitude only. |
| Stint length is mostly set by the rules, so it isn't a pure choice | In Hypercar, stint length is governed by a virtual energy allowance set per car and track by the BoP, not by fuel or tyres ([Autosport, 2023](https://www.autosport.com/imsa/news/how-virtual-energy-has-become-a-defining-principle-in-hypercar-racing/10531359/)). | Partly eases E-020's worry that long stints are self-selected. Short-filling and caution timing still make stint length a choice. The source covers Hypercars only. |
| The warm-up lap after a stop is measured on its own | Tyre warmers are banned (from 2024 at Le Mans too). Drivers put the out-lap loss on cold tyres at "over 15 s" to "20 to 30 s" at Le Mans ([Motor Sport, Jun 2024](https://www.motorsportmagazine.com/articles/sports-cars/le-mans/cold-war-why-tyre-blanket-ban-is-causing-le-mans-spins)). | Consistent. Our pit loss includes the out-lap, and lap 2 is reported separately. |
| Pit loss is in-lap + out-lap − 2 × normal lap | Teams weigh in-lap and out-lap times, pit lane length and speed limit, box position and service time, and use a "ghost car" to see where they would rejoin. This is an IMSA piece ([Sportscar365, 2019](https://sportscar365.com/imsa/iwsc/michelin-imsa-insider-pit-stop-math/)). | Consistent with the time-lost-against-staying-out definition. Teams also know service content, and we don't. |
| Typical stop figures | Porsche: fastest refuel-only stop 51.3 s and shortest stop with driver and tyre change 73.9 s, pit entry to exit, Le Mans 2015 ([Porsche, 2016](https://newsroom.porsche.com/dam/jcr:fc372d5c-da00-4e14-a972-8643c44edebf/PM_E_RaceStrategy.pdf)). Toyota at Fuji 2025: 50.7 s pit-in to pit-out with no tyres. Alpine: 63 and 67 s with some tyres ([Autosport](https://www.autosport.com/wec/news/how-an-informed-gamble-enabled-toyota-to-beat-alpine-in-the-fuji-6-hours/10859769/)). | Same order as our pit-lane times (`PIT_TIME_S`, medians about 75 to 84 s by class and driver change in 2024, E-020). These mix stops with and without tyres. |
| The extra cost with a driver change is reported as an association | Refuelling comes first. Tyre work follows with at most four people and two wheel guns, and a driver change may happen at any time during the stop ([2025 Sporting Regulations, 12.2.2, p. 40](https://www.fia.com/sites/default/files/2025_fia_world_endurance_championship_sporting_regulations_clean_v1.1.pdf)). | The regulations allow a driver change to overlap refuelling, so the change itself needn't add time. Our timing can't separate a driver change from tyre work done at the same stop, so the 13 s difference can't be attributed to either. Not tested. |
| Practice is used cautiously | Teams are suspected of hiding pace to influence BoP ([Motor Sport, Jun 2023](https://motorsportmagazine.com/articles/sports-cars/sandbagging-is-back-as-wec-tries-to-force-a-level-le-mans-playing-field)). Practice runs differ in fuel and tyres. | E-020 found no clear gap between practice and last year's race on 7 races. E-021 uses practice only as a class-wide dry reference, and only for a race that ran more than 4% slower than it. A class median doesn't remove run plans that the whole class shares, such as low fuel, so a race switches only when the gap is far bigger than such effects. |
| Caution periods are handled apart | Caution timing decides races. Under a Virtual Safety Car the pits stay open and gaps hold. Under a Full Course Yellow the pits close. Toyota won Fuji 2025 by short-filling under a safety car ([Autosport](https://www.autosport.com/wec/news/how-an-informed-gamble-enabled-toyota-to-beat-alpine-in-the-fuji-6-hours/10859769/)). | Our pit loss excludes caution stops, which is right for a green-flag cost. Layer 4 has to model the caution case itself. |

## What this doesn't establish

- Teams' internal methods aren't public. These sources are press releases, journalism and rule-makers' statements, so this shows the choices are consistent with how the sport describes its work. It doesn't show teams compute things the same way.
- The Porsche figures are from 2015 LMP1 cars and are an order of magnitude, not a benchmark.
- Seen only as search results and not used above: Michelin's comments on double and triple stints, and pieces on reading Formula 1 practice long runs.
